back when I was implementing robert's stmt.Block scopes and additional
Lox-statements, like variable-declaration, I wanted to support Lox with
multi-variable declaration syntax, so that numerous variables could be
declared on the same line of Lox-code, and parsed as a sequence of -
declaration-statements.
now, instead of doing it the experienced way and KISS (keeping it simple -
silly), I decided to reuse the Block-visitor-method in the Interpreter,
(which already had execution of numerous statements), by splitting its
functionality into 2 methods - one that executes a sequence of statements,
and one which chooses (based on a predefined boolean flag within the AST-
-node, declared by the Parser himself) whether to create a new environment -
bound to that new scope declaration, or not, and simply execute the sequence.

that was all fun and games and I totally forgot about this implementation
and remember that I was thinking to myself how this complication of the
original Lox code is actually the better and cleaner way of reusing such
functionality without extra boilerplate code in the interpreter...
and here comes the new class called the Resolver, which I didn't know about
back when I was implementing this "contract" for the stmt.Block node, which
now forces all visitor implementors to check that boolean flag, and work-
-around the now-2 different possibilities of executing this node, and of
course I forgot about this overcomplication by the time I was reimplementing
a new Block-visitor method, now in the Resolver, and left it to virtualize
the creation of a new scoped-environment, on every single call to this Block
AST-node, no matter if the Parser's varDeclaration()-method was the one to
wrap this Block-node around the Stmt.Var, and passed the correct boolean.

all of that caused a desync between the Resolver declares variables in
scopes, and the way the Interpreter is expecting them to be saved in its
locals-map of variable lookup locations in the local/global environments.

so moral of the story is to stick to KISS, and be careful in commiting to
long distance contracts before knowing or thinking about the full project
architecture as a whole.
